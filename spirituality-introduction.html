Spirituality — A Practical Path to Peace and Leadership

Introduction

This book is about spirituality as a practical matter — not as a subject for scholars or monastics, but as something an ordinary person, living an ordinary life, can actually use.

It is not a book of theology. It does not ask you to adopt a particular faith, abandon the one you have, or accept anything on authority alone. What it asks is simpler: that you bring an open mind to a set of ideas that have been tested, across centuries and across very different kinds of lives, and judge them by what they do for you.

The book is organised in four parts.

Part One makes the case that something is genuinely missing from the way most of us live — not because our lives have failed, but because the tools we reach for most naturally (a better position, a larger income, a busier schedule) do not reach the place where the real difficulty sits.

Part Two lays the groundwork: what spirituality actually is, how it differs from religion, what daily practice looks like in concrete terms, what a genuine mystic or guru is and how to recognise one, and what the path is ultimately moving toward.

Part Three takes the principles of Part Two directly into four areas where most people feel the strain most acutely — restlessness and unhappiness, stalled ambition, health, and relationships. An illustrative story runs alongside, showing what these ideas look like when they meet a real household, with real people in it.

Part Four is practice: a simple daily routine, a glossary of the key terms drawn from the traditions this book works with, and a transparent account of every source quoted in these pages.

A word on the traditions this book draws from. Kashmir Shaivism, the Bhagavad Gita, the vakhs of Lal Ded, the dohas of Kabir — these are not decoration. They are the primary sources. They are also the traditions I was raised near, and have returned to, repeatedly, across a long life. I have not approached them as an academic. I have approached them as someone who needed them to be true and found, in practice, that they largely were.

That lived experience runs through every chapter. So does a conviction that spirituality, honestly understood, is not about arranging life so that difficulty bypasses you. It is about retaining a measure of inner freedom over whatever happens — a stalled plan, a sudden loss, a strained relationship, a body that begins to ask for more attention than it used to. Not pretending the difficulty away. Not being destroyed by it either.

That is the practical path this book describes.

— Manoj Bhat
Bangalore, 2026

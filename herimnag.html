Herimnag, and the Ground Around It

There was a spring in our village, close enough that we went to it almost without deciding to. We called it Herimnag. Its water was clear and, in winter, noticeably warmer than anything around it, warm enough that fish took shelter there while the rivers stood still under ice. From it, small streams ran out in several directions across the open ground, as if the spring could not decide which part of the village to serve first, and so served all of it.

Our elders told us that we would visit it on Navreh, the Kashmiri New Year, and on Sankranti, as a tribute to the spring and to the divinity they believed lived in it. I did not question this as a child. Why would I? A spring that gave warm water when every river had frozen seemed to me then, and seems to me still, a reasonable place to look for something holy.

In those winters, a warm spring was not a picturesque thing. It was a necessity. I remember the winter of 1990, when I was on duty in Kashmir, and colleagues who knew the area took me to a spring where the water came down from higher ground in a steady stream, warm enough to bathe under in the cold. For the religious-minded, who bathed every morning before beginning their prayers, such springs made winter possible. Around many of them lay open ground, the gahcharai, kept for grazing cattle. The spring, the stream and the open land belonged together.

On this visit, I found Herimnag protected. A wall now stands around it, and an iron mesh covers the water. Someone, somewhere, had decided it was worth preserving, and I was grateful for that. But the open ground that once surrounded it was gone. It had been occupied. The spring has been saved; the space it breathed through has not.

I understand why. Piped water now reaches every home, and the winters are no longer as severe as they once were. The warm spring has quietly moved from the centre of Kashmiri life to its edge. What was once a necessity has become a monument, and monuments, it seems, need only a wall.

Whether the authorities are unaware of this, or are simply waiting for someone to complain, I cannot say. My own sense is that no complaint is coming soon. Perhaps that is the real change I noticed: not that the land was taken, but that nobody remains who feels it was theirs to protect.

— Manoj Bhat

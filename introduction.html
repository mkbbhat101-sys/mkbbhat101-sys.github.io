Kashmir and Beyond • Volume I

An Introduction

The story of my life, beginning in the small village of Chandpora, Harwan, in Kashmir, is best told through four houses.

This book is accordingly told in five parts. The first four parts follow that journey through the four houses in turn: from my earliest years in Kashmir, through the years of exile, rebuilding, and reflection that followed. Alongside the personal narrative, these parts also pause, at points, to reckon with the larger story of our community: what was lost in the exodus, the unresolved question of how history should name what happened to us, and the questions that, even now, remain open.

Part Five, titled Bugien, is a separate collection of stories, folklore, and reflections: twenty-four short stories drawn from my own life and the wider memory of our village and community, and several articles devoted to the spiritual and philosophical heritage of Kashmir. Both have stayed with me throughout. Where the first four parts trace a single life in sequence, Bugien under Part V, gathers the many smaller stories that surrounded and outlasted it. Part Five closes with a further chapter, Lessons from a Life Journey, in which I have tried to draw together, chapter by chapter, whatever this journey has left me with that feels worth passing on to a younger generation, not as instruction, but as one life’s experience offered for whatever it may be worth.

These houses were not merely structures of brick, wood, and stone. They were milestones in a journey, each shaping a different version of myself. Each house carried its own rhythm, its own lessons, and its own world.

The first house was where I was born. Life there was simple and unadorned. Roads were little more than rough tracks, electricity was uncertain, and transportation was limited enough that distances seemed far greater than they do today. Yet within that simplicity there was a certain honesty. It was a life without excess, where the smallest routines carried their own quiet weight, long before I understood why they mattered.

The second house belonged to my childhood and youth. It was here that life began to unfold in all its colours. My education took shape, friendships were formed, and my world expanded beyond the boundaries of home. These were years filled with laughter shared with my sisters, long days spent with cousins, and bonds that grew naturally among neighbours and relatives.

Our childhood was woven around nature: streams, meadows, mountain slopes, and willow groves served as our playgrounds and our teachers. Small rivulets wound their way through lush grass, while flowers added colour and fragrance to the landscape. We worked in the fields and developed a close relationship with the land that sustained us.

We spent countless hours tending cattle, running after them through fields and orchards, often imagining that they welcomed us into their own world of play. Their innocence, their ability to recognise our voices, and the way they would call out upon seeing us from a distance remain among my warmest memories.

Each season brought its own magic. Winter wrapped the valley in a blanket of snow. Autumn covered the earth with deep layers of gold and copper chinar leaves. Spring transformed the landscape into a celebration of blossoms and renewal.

These memories are not merely recollections; they are part of my being. Time may pass, places may change, and life may carry us far away, but the images of those days remain permanently etched in my mind and heart.

The journey between the second and third houses, however, was neither direct nor easy. Forced exile uprooted us from our homeland and carried us into unfamiliar landscapes. For two difficult years I continued working out of Badami Bagh while the rest of the family scattered between Dharamshala and Jammu, through tented camps and rented rooms, through a long search for the simplest kind of safety. Anything like a new life was still far from taking shape.

Dehradun became our refuge. There I rebuilt my life, cleared examinations, got married, and welcomed my first child into the world. We found warmth and friendship among the people around us and created a life despite uncertainty.

Later we moved to Kanpur, where we spent nearly two years. It was there that our son, Anmol, was born. Eventually, we moved to Delhi, the city that would become our longest and most enduring home.

The third house rose in Delhi, nearly a thousand kilometres away from everything familiar. Delhi was not merely a new destination; it was an entirely new life.

I arrived as a stranger carrying uncertainty and hope. Over time, I earned my place through work and perseverance. I became a professional, learning the value of dedication and responsibility. I became a husband, discovering the meaning of partnership, and a father, experiencing a love that reshaped my understanding of life itself.

Delhi changed more than my surroundings; it transformed me. Slowly, almost imperceptibly, I ceased to be a visitor and became a part of the city.

The fourth house now stands nearly two thousand kilometres further still, in Bangalore, built, in the end, not by me but by the next generation, on their own initiative. I had once imagined it only as a future chapter, quieter and shaped more by reflection than by pursuit. I find, now that I am actually living it, that it has turned out to be very much more than that, a place measured less by ambition than by the laughter of a grandchild and the satisfaction of looking back on a long journey.

Four houses. Four lives within one life.

The second house, however, continues to occupy a special place in my heart. It still stands, waiting silently amid memories and uncertainties. Whether life will ever return to it in the way we once knew remains unknown. Time, circumstances, and differing aspirations have created their own complexities.

Looking back, I realise that none of us imagined the course our lives would take. We had expected to spend our adult years in Kashmir, living ordinary lives in familiar surroundings. Fate, however, had other plans.

Those years brought struggle and displacement. We became like rolling stones, moving from one city to another, leaving behind neighbours and childhood friends, while forming new bonds elsewhere. We became strangers in our own homeland and yet found friendship and acceptance far beyond its borders, though neither quite made up for what we had left behind.

The experience of exile affected each of us differently. Some adapted quickly, becoming more outgoing and confident in their new surroundings. Others, like me, turned inward, finding greater comfort in reflection than in crowds.

After leaving Kashmir, we became like potted plants. We carried our roots with us, but not the soil that had nourished them for generations. We took root in Jammu, Dehradun, Kanpur, Delhi, and many other places, built new lives, and formed new relationships.

Yet a pot plant’s roots do something else too, given time: they produce seeds of their own. Perhaps it will not be us, but those seeds (our children, or theirs) who one day may find their way back to the land of Kashyap and help it grow again. Kashyap Rishi, as the Nilamata Purana records, drained the primordial lake Satisar to make the Valley habitable; Kashmir itself takes its name from him.

Why these events happened and who bears responsibility are questions that history may continue to debate. For those who lived through them, however, the reality was simple: lives were disrupted, homes were lost, and an entire community was forced to rebuild itself from the ground up.

Yet adversity also brought resilience. It strengthened our determination to preserve our values, traditions, and identity, and gave us hope. My generation spent much of its life navigating this struggle. The younger generation now carries the torch forward, armed with education, ambition, and hope.

As I bring out these recollections, I am conscious that my life has contained many more joyful moments, meaningful experiences, and events of lasting significance than those recorded in these pages. This memoir captures only those memories that have remained vivid enough to be recalled and narrated at this stage of my life: it is obviously a part of my journey, not its entirety, and an account primarily of events that happened around me.

I believe that even this partial account conveys something of the beauty, struggles, values, and transformations that I have witnessed. Through these memories, future generations may gain a glimpse of how life evolved over the course of nearly a century: how people lived, worked, celebrated, endured hardships, and adapted to change.

It brings to mind the old story of the blind men and the elephant: to one it was a pole, to another a wall, to yet another a rope, and to the last one a fan. Each was far from the whole truth. Mine, too, is the situation of the blind man, aware of only part of the truth, because each of us can witness only the small part of life unfolding around us: what was happening just a few metres away was often hardly known to us. Electronic media and mobile phones have since made us aware of every detail we care to seek out, but no such thing existed in those days. Besides, there was an information war going on of which we, as ordinary citizens, had little real awareness, which may itself be part of what this account is for: to set down, at least, the part of the elephant that I could feel.

It is, thus, an honest record of what I witnessed, experienced, heard, and understood from where I stood. Where I have relied on memory alone, or on what was told to me by others, I have tried to say so within these pages, rather than presenting recollection as settled fact. Where I have offered my own opinion rather than established history, I have tried to be equally plain about that. A few names have been changed to protect the privacy of people who did not choose to be written about.

Expressing one’s feelings, one’s pain, without fear, is the first step towards healing and the beginning of reconciliation.

It is my sincere hope that each generation will preserve and record its own experiences for those who follow. Such personal accounts form a living bridge between the past and the future: between the family we leave these pages to and the valley that shaped us, between what was nearly lost and what we refused to let go.

They are among the most precious gifts that parents can leave for their children, grandparents for their grandchildren, and one generation for the next. A legacy not of wealth, but of memory, wisdom, and lived experience. Every memory set down here is a quiet insistence that what we lived through, and what we preserved, deserves to be remembered. That is the only bridge I know how to build. I have spent these pages building it.

— Manoj Bhat

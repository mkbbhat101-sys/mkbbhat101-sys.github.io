Rurban: Bringing the Village Home

Introduction: Why I Wrote This Book

One day I was going through a magazine and came across an article on interior design. The house in the pictures was full of greenery. Some plants stood on the floor, others hung from the walls. There was water flowing down a wall, a small fountain in the hall, and an air purifier humming in the corner. It looked very nice.

Then something struck me. Outside, nature gives us forests, ponds, mountains, waterfalls, grasslands, open spaces, fresh air and sunlight, all for free. In our cities, much of this has shrunk, been fenced off, or moved far from where most people live. A park may be an hour's drive away; a river may be too dirty to touch. The air is polluted, the water is polluted, the trees are vanishing. So now we are trying to recreate them inside our four walls. We look for plants that can live without sunlight. We build fountains and waterfalls in our rooms. We buy machines to make the air fresh.

Why are we doing this? Because we can no longer find these things outside.

I have a yearning for greenery, for fresh water, for forests with birds chirping inside them. For me, that is real luxury. It is in my blood. I grew up in Chandpora, in Kashmir, among springs, streams and orchards. After we left in 1990, city life became my life, and since 2000 Delhi has been my home. I have seen both lives, the village and the city, and this book comes from standing between the two.

The government has a scheme called the Rurban Mission, which takes city facilities to the villages. I want to do the opposite: bring the village to the city. Not its hardships, not the lack of roads, hospitals and jobs, or the struggle of rural life, but its habits and its rhythms. Rurbanisation, as I mean it, is about reclaiming a life lived in partnership with nature, instead of buying plastic copies of it and machines to shield us from the concrete. It means living with the earth instead of against it, and learning from the ways people once lived close to it, used what they had, and depended on one another. That is the purpose of this book.

Rurbanisation works at three levels. The first is the city, and it belongs to those who plan it: how much land is left open, how rivers and lakes are protected, how people travel, how much concrete we pour. The second is the neighbourhood: the colony, the lane, the group of flats, where people can protect a park, plant a road or celebrate a festival together. The third is the home, and it belongs to each one of us: the creeper on the wall, the leaves turned into manure, the meal shared with neighbours, the phone kept away at night. Each level needs the others. This book speaks to all three, but mostly to the home and the neighbourhood, because that is where every one of us can begin today.

I should say clearly what this book is not. I am not an authority on the environment, on city planning or on plants. I write as one citizen, and this book is my small contribution, a kind of awareness campaign from my side. I know many people already care about these things and are taking steps in their own homes and colonies. What I hope is to take it one step further: to make living with nature something people want to do, even something fashionable, the way green walls and air purifiers have become fashionable today.

This book moves through three landscapes. The first is the village I remember, where nature was part of everyday life and gave us much for free. The second is the city I have come to know, with its comforts but also its pollution, noise and shrinking green spaces. The third is about the small ways back: a creeper on a wall and a kitchen garden, leaves turned into manure, rain caught and soil kept open, houses that breathe, room for birds and animals, neighbours who share, and in the end, a city that plans for nature. Most of these I have tried in my own home in Delhi, and anyone can begin them. Running through all three is one simple idea: breath. A city that has forgotten how to breathe, an earth that needs room to breathe, and a home that can still breathe, if we let it.

We cannot all go back to the village. But we can bring the village to us.

Manoj Bhat
Delhi, 2026

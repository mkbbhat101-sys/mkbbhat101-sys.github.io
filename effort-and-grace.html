Effort and Grace: A Simple Table

We often speak of effort and destiny as if one must win over the other. Either everything is in our hands, or whatever is written will happen. In my experience, neither is true on its own. Individual effort is not always enough; something beyond us, call it grace, destiny or divine effort, also plays its part. But that divine effort does not act in a vacuum. It is awakened by our own effort.

To make this easier to see, give each kind of effort a simple value: +1 when it is positive, 0 when it is neutral, and –1 when it works against us. Adding the two gives nine possible situations.

9–1–1–2The downward spiral. Wrong action meets hard times, and each worsens the other.

Green shows a gain, grey shows no change, and red shows a loss. The deeper the colour, the stronger the result.

What the table tells us

Look down the first column and a pattern appears. When individual effort is positive, the total never falls below zero, whatever destiny does. When it is negative, the total never rises above zero, even with full divine support. Effort does not decide how high we rise; that depends partly on grace. But it decides the floor beneath us.

The table also answers a question that troubles many of us: why does the same effort bring such different results? Look at rows 1, 2 and 3. The individual effort is identical in all three, a full +1. Yet the outcomes are +2, +1 and 0. Nothing in the person changed; only the second column did. The same is true of rows 4 to 6, and of rows 7 to 9. Two people can work equally hard, with equal honesty, and one succeeds brilliantly while the other only just holds on. The difference lies in the element of uncertainty that no planning removes, the factor the traditions call destiny or grace. Seeing this clearly is a relief. It means a poor result does not always prove poor effort, and a good result does not always prove that we alone earned it.

It follows that every plan, whether a family's budget or a company's five-year strategy, must leave room for the second column. Good planners already do this, even if they would not use the word destiny. They keep a reserve for the year that goes wrong, they prepare more than one scenario, and they never commit everything to a single outcome. In thirty-four years of government accounts, I saw that the budgets which survived were rarely the most ambitious ones; they were the ones that allowed for what no one could foresee. A plan that assumes the second column will always be favourable is not confident. It is fragile. Humility before uncertainty is not a weakness in planning; it is part of planning well.

The second column is not in our hands. We cannot raise divine effort by wishing for it. Yet the table, read together with lived experience, suggests the one thing we can do: keep our own effort positive, so that grace has a door through which to enter. In row 4, grace arrives without effort, and most of us know how such windfalls go. The money is spent, the opportunity passes, the position is lost, because nothing in us was ready to hold it.

When effort itself turns negative

The hardest rows to accept are 7, 8 and 9, because in each of them we are working, sometimes very hard, and still making things worse. How can effort be negative?

Effort turns negative when it moves in the wrong direction, however much energy it carries. In my observation this happens most often when:

  • Ego drives the work. We act to prove ourselves rather than to do the task well, and we stop listening.
  • We are attached to the result. Fear of failure makes us hasty, anxious or dishonest, and we cut corners to force an outcome.
  • We act in anger or in comparison. Decisions taken to win an argument, or to match someone else, rarely serve us.
  • We push past the point of wisdom. Effort becomes stubbornness: continuing on a road that has already shown it leads nowhere.
  • We harm others along the way. Gains made at someone else's cost tend to return as losses later, in one form or another.

Notice that none of these is laziness. Negative effort is often busier than positive effort. What it lacks is awareness.

Turning negative effort around

If effort turns negative through a lack of awareness, the remedy begins with awareness too.

  • Pause before acting. A few breaths before an important decision can be the difference between –1 and +1.
  • Practise sakshi bhav, witness consciousness. Step back and observe your own motives as if they belonged to someone else. Is this action coming from clarity, or from ego, fear or anger?
  • Let go of the fruit. The teaching of Gita 2.47, that our right is to action alone and never to its fruits, is not a call to stop caring. It frees the mind from the fear that drives so much negative effort.
  • Seek honest counsel. A trusted friend, elder or teacher can often see what we cannot.
  • Correct course quickly. When we notice we are in row 7 or 8, the step from –1 to 0 is often just to stop: stop the argument, the shortcut, the harm. From 0, positive effort can begin again.
  • Repair what you can. Where negative effort has hurt others, an honest apology or a small act of amends restores more than we expect.

Conclusion

The table is simple, but it carries a teaching the traditions have always known. We are not the sole authors of our lives, and we are not helpless either. Destiny may decide how far we rise, but effort, honest and aware effort, decides whether we stand on firm ground when it does not favour us, and whether we are ready to receive it when it does. Of the two columns, only one is ours to fill. The wisdom of a spiritual life lies in filling it well, and leaving the other to grace.

And only awareness lets us live with this. Without it, a good outcome makes us proud and possessive, as though we alone had earned it, and a poor one makes us bitter, as though the world had cheated us. With awareness, we can see each result for what it is: our own effort, met by a factor beyond us. We can accept the outcome without clinging to it when it is good, and without being crushed by it when it is not. That is not resignation. It is the freedom to keep working, whatever the second column brings.
